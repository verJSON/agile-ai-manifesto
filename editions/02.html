01 / THE IDEA

More than
faster code.

We are discovering better ways to build and operate software by working with autonomous agents, learning from customers, and improving the systems that turn intent into outcomes.

Software development is changing. Agents can take meaningful action, collaborate, challenge decisions, and help carry work from idea to delivery. Our task is to build the conditions that make that autonomy useful, accountable, and worth trusting.

03 / HOW WE WORK

The thirteen
principles.

Ideas to guide the systems we build—and the way we build them.

01 / 13

Deliver outcomes users value.

Define success in terms of customer needs and observed results. Working, tested software is evidence of progress; it is not the entire outcome.

02 / 13

Welcome change and new evidence.

Design for change. Make decisions testable, corrections affordable, and reversibility a deliberate design goal.

03 / 13

Make intent actionable.

A story is not a specification. Resolve material ambiguity into explicit decisions, acceptance criteria, and boundaries before implementation. People and agents must have authority to make the decisions assigned to them; unresolved questions must reach someone empowered to answer quickly.

04 / 13

Own the specification.

The versioned specification is the source of truth for intended behavior and delegated authority. Give it an accountable owner, test it against evidence, and change it when reality proves it wrong. When conforming software fails because the specification was inadequate, correct the specification and the decisions behind it, not just the code. Where systems exchange intent, decisions, or evidence, use versioned, machine-readable contracts that carry them and identify who produced them. A contract that carries only status is not a contract for intent, decisions, or evidence, and a free-text side record does not supply what the contract must carry.

05 / 13

Give agents meaningful autonomy.

Equip agents with context, tools, distinct identities, and scoped authority to investigate, implement, review, decide, and merge work within the policies that govern them. An identity is distinct when an issuer other than the agent binds the agent run to the person or system it acts for, records the model it runs on as verified or unverified, and others can check it. Authority is scoped when it is limited to named actions and enforced where the action happens. A session label, a commit trailer, or a prompt instruction does not establish identity, and a credential the agent can merely reach is not delegated authority, even if it works; limit what it can do where the action happens.

06 / 13

Evaluate continuously and independently.

Automate testing, security analysis, and adversarial review throughout delivery. No author, human or agent, is the final judge of their own work. A finding is blocking when its reviewer says so, and only the reviewer or someone independent of the author may downgrade or dismiss it; the author may dismiss a non-blocking comment with a stated reason. Independence is shown by evidence, not by a different name: a reviewer acting for the same person as the author is not independent of the author, and the person the author acts for is not independent of the author, separate sessions alone do not show independence or model diversity, shared-model risk is disclosed, and a reviewer whose identity cannot be checked is recorded as unverified. Evaluate the checks as rigorously as the results: people and agents optimize for what you measure. A required check that did not run, or produced no verifiable result of its evaluation, has not passed; hold the work it guards and escalate. A check that does not apply to the work is not required for it, does not hold it, and is never counted as evidence about the work. Any change to a check gets at least the scrutiny of the work it guards; weakening a check, or widening who may bypass it, is high-consequence.

07 / 13

Move at the speed evidence supports.

Replace routine approval queues with evidence-driven evaluation. The consequences and reversibility of a change determine the scrutiny it receives and who may clear it; some evidence takes time to obtain. Routine work is reversible, and evidence shows its failure is bounded and cheap to detect and undo; an independent reviewer, human or agent, can clear it. Work is high-consequence when it is hard to reverse, when security, legal, or customer harm could occur before it can be detected and undone, or when it widens who may act or weakens or removes a required check; its named human owner decides it. Passing checks do not by themselves make work routine. When the class is uncertain, treat the work as high-consequence.

08 / 13

Explore architecture through evidence.

Model alternatives and evaluate them using experiments, simulations, operational data, and explicit constraints. Customer needs, security, legal obligations, operations, and cost will conflict. Make material constraints and their owners explicit, and resolve conflicts deliberately; agents must not settle them by accident. Record objectives, trade-offs, assumptions, and the reasons behind decisions.

09 / 13

Make consequential actions traceable.

Record who or what acted, under whose authority, and on what evidence. Every consequential action must trace to a responsible person or organization. High-consequence decisions must have a named human owner, who accepts, rejects, or returns the specific recommendation once it is known and before it takes effect. Agreeing in advance to follow a recommendation is delegation, not ownership of the decision. Make claims about identity, authority, provenance, and outcomes verifiable with signed attestations from an issuer that whoever relies on them trusts independently of the message. A signature shows who vouched for a claim, not that the action was permitted or that the outcome happened. Check a new action against current authority, a past action against the authority that applied when it was taken, and an outcome against evidence. What an actor, or the tooling that runs it, reports about itself, including a reviewing model's name, is a claim, not an attestation. Record what cannot be verified as unverified, keeping what was observed.

10 / 13

Learn from real use.

Product decisions must answer to customer feedback, adoption, operational telemetry, and business outcomes. Run experiments to challenge assumptions; correlation is not demonstrated value.

11 / 13

Keep the system understandable.

Choose the simplest design that meets the need. Cheap code generation makes unnecessary features, dependencies, and abstractions easier to create, not cheaper to maintain.

12 / 13

Optimize the whole flow.

Judge the whole outcome against stated goals and a baseline: customer value, quality, latency, operating cost, compute, and the capacity to evaluate and maintain the work. Token efficiency is a means, not a goal. A saving counts only when a recorded comparison shows it improves that whole outcome, for example the same quality at lower cost; an inconclusive comparison is recorded as inconclusive, not as an improvement. A saving never counts when it weakens required verification, traceability, or quality.

13 / 13

Improve the factory continuously.

Use failures, incidents, reviews, and customer feedback to improve agents, specifications, evaluation, architecture, and governance. People and agents must be able to surface concerns and challenge assumptions. Keep material dissent, and how it was resolved, in the record. When evaluators do not converge, give the disagreement and the options to the accountable owner instead of averaging it away. A time limit, a missing vote, silence, or agreement among evaluators is not by itself proof of correctness, nor a grant of authority. A recorded dissent is a right to be heard, not by itself a veto.

04 / THE CONNECTIVE TISSUE

Shared language.
Lasting evidence.

Autonomy works better when systems understand each other.

Versioned, machine-readable contracts give agents and system tiers a shared language for intent, decisions, evidence, and who produced them. Explicit versions make change visible, keep the meaning of past records, and support compatibility, audit and continuous improvement. A receiver rejects versions and kinds it does not recognize instead of guessing their meaning. A message that passes its schema is well formed, not true: free text can explain, but it never moves work into an approved or finished state, and a claim about who produced a message stays a claim until it is verified.

A direction does not lower the bar: a contract that carries only status is not yet a contract for intent, decisions, and evidence. Read more about versioned contracts and audit evidence.
